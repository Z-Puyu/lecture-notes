\chapter{Static Games of Complete Information}
\section{Nash Equilibrium}
We start with a classic story:
\begin{genbox}{Prisoners' Dilemma}
    Two suspects are arrested and charged with a crime. The police lack sufficient evidence to convict the suspects, unless at least one confesses. The suspects are held in separate cells and told:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If only one of you confesses and testifies against your
        partner, then the person who confesses will go free while
        the person who does not confess will be convicted and
        given a $20$-year jail sentence.
        \item If both of you confess, then both will be convicted and sent to prison for $5$ years.
        \item Finally, if neither of you confesses, then both of you will be convicted of a minor offence and sentenced to $1$ year in jail .
    \end{itemize} 
    What should the suspects do?    
\end{genbox}
To investigate the situation, we can represent the prisoners' decisions using a matrix:
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{bNiceMatrix}[first-row,first-col]
        & \textrm{Holdout} & \textrm{Confess} \\
        \textrm{Holdout} & \left(-1, -1\right) & \left(-20, 0\right) \\
        \textrm{Confess} & \left(0, -20\right) & \left(-5, -5\right)
    \end{bNiceMatrix}.
\end{equation*}
With some observation, it is easy to see that no matter what they other prisoner chooses to do, the best decision for the prisoner is to confess. Note that in this representation, we represent a \textit{strategy} with an ordered pair consisting of the decisions made by the two prisoners respectively. Extending this idea to a higher dimension, we see that every strategy of a game is formed by an ordered $n$-tuple, where each factor represents a decision taken by the corresponding player.
\begin{dfnbox}{Strategy Space}{strategy}
    In an $n$-player game, the {\color{red} \textbf{strategy space}} of the $i$-th player, denoted by $S_i$, is the set of all possible decisions for the player.
\end{dfnbox}
In the above example, for each strategy $\left(i, j\right)$, we then place the years of imprisonment, or the \textit{payoff}, for the two prisoners following that strategy in the corresponding matrix entry. Intuitively, for an $n$-player game in general, we can assign a payoff value for each strategy in $\prod_{i = 1}^nS_i$, the set of all strategies. 
\begin{dfnbox}{Payoff Function}{payoff}
    Let $S_i$ be the strategy space of the $i$-th player in an $n$-player game, the {\color{red} \textbf{payoff function}} for the $i$-th player is a map $u_i \colon \prod_{j = 1}^nS_j \to \R$.
\end{dfnbox}
This gives us a convenient way to represent a game quantitatively.
\begin{dfnbox}{Normal-Form of Games}{normalForm}
    Let $G$ be an $n$-player game. Let $S_i$ be the strategy space and $u_i$ be the payoff function of the $i$-th player, then the {\color{red} \textbf{normal-form}}, or {\color{red} \textbf{strategic form}} of $G$ is defined as 
    \begin{equation*}
        G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{dfnbox}
It is important to note that the payoff a player depends not only on his own action but also on the actions of others. This inter-dependence is the
essence of games.
\begin{notebox}
    \begin{remark}
        For $2$-player games, the payoff can be represented in a matrix.
    \end{remark}
\end{notebox}
Given any game, our goal is to derive the optimal strategy for the players. Before that, we shall discuss some criteria under which a strategy cannot be optimal. For the sake of notational simplicity, we denote 
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbfit{s}_{-i} \coloneqq \left(s_1, \cdots, s_{i - 1}, s_{i + 1}, \cdots, s_n\right)
\end{equation*}
and 
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbfit{s} \coloneqq \left(s_1, s_2, \cdots, s_n\right) = \left(s_i, s_{-i}\right).
\end{equation*}
Analogously, we denote
\begin{equation*}
    S_{-i} \coloneqq \prod_{j = 1}^{i - 1}S_j \times \prod_{j = i + 1}^nS_j.
\end{equation*}
\begin{dfnbox}{Dominated and Dominant Strategies}{dominate}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}$ be a normal-form game and $s_i, s_i' \in S_i$ be two strategies for the $i$-th player. We say that $s_i$ is {\color{red} \textbf{strictly dominated}} by $s_i'$ or that $s_i'$ is {\color{red} \textbf{strictly dominant}} over $s_i$ if 
    \begin{equation*}
        u_i\left(s_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right) < u_i\left(s'_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right) \quad \textrm{for all } \mathbfit{s}_{-i} \in S_{-i}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{dfnbox}
\begin{notebox}
    \begin{remark}
        In other words, $s_i$ is strictly dominated by $s_i'$ if and only if it is a strictly worse decision no matter what the other players decide to do.
    \end{remark}
\end{notebox}
If a strategy $s$ is strictly dominated by any other strategy, then it is not a rational strategy because it can never be optimal.

Assuming that in an $n$-player game, every player knows exactly what the strategies are and their respective payoffs, then we can proceed with the following reasoning:
\begin{tecbox}{Iterated Elimination of Strictly Dominated Strategies (IESDS)}{IESDS}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Suppose that $s_{i_0}$ is strictly dominated by any other strategy of player $i$, then if the player is rational, the strategy will never be played.
        \item Therefore, we can reduce the strategy space to $\left\{\mathbfit{s} \in \prod_{i = 1}^nS_i \colon s_i \neq s_{i_0}\right\}$.
        \item Apply the same reasoning on the reduced strategy space iteratively. If at each iteration there exists such a strictly dominated strategy, then we can successfully obtain the optimal strategy for the $n$-players.
    \end{itemize}
\end{tecbox}
However, in most games, we might not be able to find such a strictly dominated strategy. Therefore, we have to think from a conditional context: given a set of strategies played by the other players, what is the \textit{best response} for the current player?
\begin{dfnbox}{Best-Response Correspondence}{bestResponse}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}$ be a normal-form game. The {\color{red} \textbf{best-response correspondence}} for player $i$ is a map $R_i \colon S_{-i} \to \mathcal{P}\left(S_i\right)$ defined by 
    \begin{equation*}
        R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right) \coloneqq \argmax_{s_i \in S_i}u_i\left(s_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right).
    \end{equation*}
\end{dfnbox}
Now, given $\mathbfit{s}_{-i}$, the actions played by the other players, $R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right)$ tells us what the best responses are for player $i$ for his own profit. In other words, player $i$ has \textbf{no incentive} to choose any strategy in $S_i \setminus R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right)$. If such a situation holds for all the players, then it is clear that the game has reached an \textit{equilibrium} where no player is incentivised to deviated from the current strategy.

It is clear that such equilibriums occur exactly when the strategies of all players are the best responses of one another.
\begin{dfnbox}{Nash Equilibrium}{NashEqui}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}$ be a normal-form $n$-player game, then the strategies~$\left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right)$ are a {\color{red} \textbf{Nash equilibrium}} if 
    \begin{equation*}
        s_i^* \in R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) \quad\textrm{for all } i = 1, 2, \cdots, n, 
    \end{equation*}
    or equivalently, 
    \begin{equation*}
        u_i\left(s_i^*, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) = \max_{s_i \in S_i}u_i\left(s_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) \quad\textrm{for all } i = 1, 2, \cdots, n.
    \end{equation*}
\end{dfnbox}
Now, define 
\begin{equation*}
    G\left(R_i\right) \coloneqq \left\{\left(s_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right) \colon s_i \in R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right), \mathbfit{s}_{-i} \in S_{-i}\right\}
\end{equation*}
as the \textbf{graph} of $R_i$, then one can check that $\left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right)$ are a Nash equilibrium if and only if 
\begin{equation*}
    \left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right) \in \bigcap_{i = 1}^n G\left(R_i\right).
\end{equation*}
Next, let us consider a simple case of bi-matrix games. Take any guess $\left(s_1, s_2\right) \in S_1 \times S_2$. We can compute $R_2\left(s_1\right)$ and $R_1\left(s_2\right)$, and $\left(s_1, s_2\right)$ are a Nash equilibrium if and only if $s_1 \in R_1\left(s_2\right)$ and $s_2 \in R_2\left(s_1\right)$ by Definition \ref{dfn:NashEqui}.

Therefore, we can simply repeat this calculation for every pair $\left(s_1, s_2\right) \in S_1 \times S_2$ and deduce the set $G\left(R_1\right) \cap G\left(R_2\right)$, which consists of all Nash equilibria.
\begin{probox}{Relation between Nash Equilibrium and IESDS}{iesdsNash}
    Let $G = \left(S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right)$ be an $n$-player normal-form game. If the strategies $\left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right)$ are a Nash equilibrium, then each $s_i^*$ cannot be eliminated by IESDS.
    \tcblower
    \begin{proof}
        Suppose on contrary that there exists some $s_i^*$ which is eliminated by IESDS. Without loss of generality, assume $s_i^*$ to be the first in $\left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right)$ to be eliminated. By Technique \ref{tec:IESDS}, there exists some $s_i' \in S_i$ with $s_i' \neq s_i^*$ such that 
        \begin{equation*}
            u_i\left(s_i^*, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right) < u_i\left(s_i', \mathbfit{s}_{-i}\right)
        \end{equation*}
        for all $\mathbfit{s}_{-i} \in S_{-i}$. In particular, this means that 
        \begin{equation*}
            u_i\left(s_i^*, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) < u_i\left(s_i', \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right).
        \end{equation*}
        However, by Definition \ref{dfn:NashEqui}, we have $u_i\left(s_i^*, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) = \max_{s_i \in S_i}u_i\left(s_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right)$, which is a contradiction.
    \end{proof}
\end{probox}
Let $S$ be the resultant strategy space obtained from IESDS and $N$ be the set of all Nash equilibria of a game, then the above proposition essentially means that $N \subseteq S$. 

Note that this does not mean that Proposition \ref{pro:iesdsNash} establishes the \textbf{existence} of Nash equilibria for any game. However, we can indeed conclude a little bit about the uniqueness of Nash equilibrium under some circumstances.
\begin{probox}{Uniqueness of Nash Equilibrium from IESDS}{IESDSNash}
    Let $G = \left(S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right)$ be an $n$-player normal-form game with finite strategy spaces. If $\left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right) \in S$ are the only strategies which are not eliminated by IESDS, then it is the unique Nash equilibrium.
    \tcblower
    \begin{proof}
        The uniqueness is obvious from Proposition \ref{pro:iesdsNash}, so it suffices to prove that the strategies $\left(s_1^*, s_2^*, \cdots, s_n^*\right)$ are indeed a Nash equilibrium. Suppose on contrary that it is not a Nash equilibrium, then there exists some $s_i^*$ such that $s_i^* \notin R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right)$. Take some $b_i \in R_i\left(\mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right)$, then 
        \begin{equation*}
            u_i\left(b_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) > u_i\left(s_i^*, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right).
        \end{equation*}
        However, since $b_i$ is eliminated, it must be strictly dominated by some $t_i \in S_i$ such that $t_i \neq b_i$. Therefore, we have 
        \begin{equation*}
            u_i\left(t_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right) > u_i\left(b_i, \mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*\right)
        \end{equation*}
        because $\mathbfit{s}_{-i}^*$ are strategies which are not eliminated, but this is a contradiction since~$t_i$ is a better response than $b_i$.
    \end{proof}
\end{probox}
So far, in our analysis we have been assuming that the players in a game make decisions \textbf{deterministically}, i.e., the player always choose a single strategy as the response. However, in real-life scenarios, there could be some random factors involved in decision-making.
\begin{dfnbox}{Mixed Strategy}{mixedStrategy}
    Let $G = \left(S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right)$ be an $n$-player normal-form game with finite strategy spaces. Suppose $S_i = \left\{s_{i1}, s_{i2}, \cdots, s_{ik}\right\}$, then each $s_{ij} \in S_i$ is a {\color{red} \textbf{pure strategy}} for player $i$ and a probability distribution $p_i \coloneqq \left(p_{i1}, p_{i2}, \cdots, p_{ik}\right)$ is said to be a {\color{red} \textbf{mixed strategy}} such that the probability of player $i$ playing $s_{ij}$ is $p_{ij}$.
\end{dfnbox}
As an example, we consider two players, $i$ and $j$, with $M$ and $N$ pure strategies respectively. Let $p_i$ and~$p_j$ be mixed strategies for $i$ and $j$ respectively, then the \textbf{expected payoff} of player~$i$ playing any pure strategy $s_{ik}$ is 
\begin{equation*}
    v_i\left(s_{ik}, p_j\right) \coloneqq \sum_{n = 1}^{N}p_{jn}u_i\left(s_{ik}, s_{jn}\right).
\end{equation*}
With this, we can compute the \textbf{expected payoff} for the mixed strategy $p_i$ as 
\begin{align*}
    v_i\left(p_i, p_j\right) & \coloneqq \sum_{m = 1}^{M}p_{im}v_i\left(s_{im}, p_j\right) \\
    & = \sum_{m = 1}^{M}\sum_{n = 1}^{N}p_{im}p_{jn}u_i\left(s_{im}, s_{jn}\right).
\end{align*}
Similarly, the expected payoff for $p_j$ is 
\begin{equation*}
    v_j\left(p_j, p_i\right) = \sum_{n = 1}^{N}\sum_{m = 1}^{M}p_{jn}p_{im}u_j\left(s_{jn}, s_{im}\right).
\end{equation*}
Now we can extend the notion of Nash equilibrium to mixed strategies using expected payoffs.
\begin{dfnbox}{Nash Equilibrium for Mixed Strategies}{NashEquiMixed}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}$ be a normal-form $n$-player game, then the probability distributions $\left(p_1^*, p_2^*, \cdots, p_n^*\right)$ are a {\color{red} \textbf{Nash equilibrium}} if 
    \begin{equation*}
        v_i\left(p_i^*, \mathbfit{p}_{-i}^*\right) \geq v_i\left(p_i, \mathbfit{p}_{-i}^*\right)
    \end{equation*}
    for any probability distribution $p_i$ on $S_i$.
\end{dfnbox}
Intuitively, if a pure strategy $s_{ik}$ is eliminated by IESDS, then any mixed strategy that is good enough probably should not favour playing the strategy. Indeed, we have the following result:
\begin{probox}{Zero Probability of Strategies Eliminated by IESDS}{zeroProbIESDS}
    Let $\left(p_1^*, p_2^*, \cdots, p_n^*\right)$ be any mixed strategy Nash equilibrium. If $s_{ik}$ is eliminated by IESDS, then $p_{ik}^* = 0$.
\end{probox}
Lastly, combining the two scenarios of Nash equilibria, the following theorem states formally the existence of Nash equilibria:
\begin{thmbox}{Nash's Theorem}{Nash}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}$ be a normal-form $n$-player game with finite strategy spaces, then there exists at least one Nash equilibrium if mixed strategies are allowed.
\end{thmbox}
\section{Economic Models}
Suppose that there are two firms producing the same product. Let $q_1$ and $q_2$ be the quantity produced by the two firms respectively and $c$ be the unit cost of production. Assuming that the demand is a linear function in price, then there exists positive constants $q_0$ and $k$ such that 
\begin{equation*}
    Q\left(P\right) = q_0 - kP,
\end{equation*}
where $Q$ is the demand and $P$ is the price. We can assume that $P = 0$ when $Q \geq q_0$, so we have 
\begin{equation*}
    P\left(Q\right) = \begin{cases}
        0 & \textrm{if } Q \geq q_0 \\
        a - bQ & \textrm{otherwise}
    \end{cases},
\end{equation*}
where $a = \frac{q_0}{k}$ and $b = \frac{1}{k}$. Notice that this means that when the price reaches $a$, the demand for the product vanishes.
\begin{dfnbox}{Demand-Side Reservation Price}{demandSideReservation}
    The {\color{red} \textbf{demand-side reservation price}} is the lowest price at which the demand is $0$.
\end{dfnbox}
Here our goal is to achieve \textit{market clearing price}.
\begin{dfnbox}{Market Clearing Price}{marketClear}
    The {\color{red} \textbf{market clearing price}} is the price at which the demand is equal to the supply.
\end{dfnbox}
In order to achieve the market clearing price, the demand should always be equal to the supply, so the firm should produce such that $Q = q_1 + q_2$. This means that 
\begin{equation*}
    P\left(Q\right) = \begin{cases}
        0 & \textrm{if } Q \geq q_0 \\
        a - bq_1 - bq_2 & \textrm{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
Define the profit by 
\begin{equation*}
    \pi_i\left(q_1, q_2\right) = \bigl(P\left(Q\right) - c\bigr)q_i
\end{equation*}
for $i = 1, 2$, where $c$ is the unit cost of production, then we have 
\begin{equation*}
    \pi_i\left(q_1, q_2\right) = \begin{cases}
        \left(a - bq_1 - bq_2 - c\right)q_i & \textrm{if } Q < q_0 \\
        -cq_1 & \textrm{if } Q \geq q_0
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation*}
\begin{dfnbox}{Cournot Model of Duopoly}{Cournot}
    Let $S_1, S_2 \coloneqq \left[0, \infty\right)$ be possible supply quantities for a product produced by two firms. Assuming linear demand such that the demand is $q_0$ at a price of $0$, then there exists positive constants $a$ and $b$ such that
    \begin{equation*}
        \pi_i\left(q_1, q_2\right) = \begin{cases}
            \left(a - bq_1 - bq_2 - c\right)q_i & \textrm{if } Q < q_0 \\
            -cq_1 & \textrm{if } Q \geq q_0
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    for $i = 1, 2$, where $q_1 \in S_1, q_2 \in S_2$ and $Q = q_1 + q_2$. The $2$-player normal-form game~$G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \pi_1, \pi_2\right\}$ is known as the {\color{red} \textbf{Cournot model of duopoly}}. 
\end{dfnbox}
It is clear that if $q_1 > q_0 - q_2$, then $\pi_1\left(q_1, q_2\right) = -cq_1 < 0$ so it cannot be an optimum. Therefore, we effectively only need to consider $q_1 \in \left[0, q_0 - q_2\right]$ and $q_2 \in \left[0, q_0 - q_1\right]$.
\begin{probox}{Best Response in Cournot Model of Duopoly}{CournotBestResponse}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \pi_1, \pi_2\right\}$ be a Cournot model of duopoly, then for any $q_2 \in S_2$, the best response of the first firm is 
    \begin{equation*}
        q_1 \coloneqq R_1\left(q_2\right) = \begin{cases}
            \frac{1}{2b}\left(a - bq_2 - c\right) & \textrm{if } q_2 \leq \frac{a - c}{b} \\
            0 & \textrm{if } q_2 > \frac{a - c}{b}
        \end{cases}.
    \end{equation*}
    \tcblower
    \begin{proof}
        Fix $q_2 \geq 0$ as the output of the second firm. Note that it suffices to consider the case where $\pi_1\left(q_1, q_2\right) = \left(a - bq_1 - bq_2 - c\right)q_1$. If $a - c - bq_2 < 0$, for any $q_1 > 0$, we have $\pi_1\left(q_1, q_2\right) < 0$ so $q_1 = 0$ is the best response. Otherwise, consider 
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q_1} = -2bq_1 + a - bq_2 - c = 0.
        \end{equation*} 
        Solving the equation yields 
        \begin{equation*}
            q_1 = \frac{a - bq_2 - c}{2b}.
        \end{equation*}
        Therefore, the best response of the first firm is 
        \begin{equation*}
            R_1\left(q_2\right) = \begin{cases}
                \frac{1}{2b}\left(a - bq_2 - c\right) & \textrm{if } q_2 \leq \frac{a - c}{b} \\
                0 & \textrm{if } q_2 > \frac{a - c}{b}
            \end{cases}.
        \end{equation*}
    \end{proof}
\end{probox}
\begin{notebox}
    \begin{remark}
        In a simplified case where $Q\left(P\right) = q_0 - P$ (i.e., $k = 1$), we have $a = q_0$ and~$b = 1$, and so 
        \begin{equation*}
            R_1\left(q_2\right) = \begin{cases}
                \frac{1}{2}\left(a - q_2 - c\right) & \textrm{if } q_2 \leq a - c \\
                0 & \textrm{if } q_2 > a - c
            \end{cases}.
        \end{equation*}
    \end{remark}
\end{notebox}
Using the above result, it is easy to compute the Nash equilibrium of the Cournot model by solving the system 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{cases}
        q_1^* & = \frac{1}{2b}\left(a - bq_2^* - c\right) \\
        q_2^* & = \frac{1}{2b}\left(a - bq_1^* - c\right)
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation*}
\begin{corbox}{Nash Equilibrium of Cournot Model of Duopoly}{CournotEqui}
    In a Cournot model of duopoly, the Nash equilibrium is $q_1^* = q_2^* = \frac{1}{3b}\left(a - c\right)$.
\end{corbox}
Next, instead of \textbf{homogeneous product}, let us consider firms producing \textbf{differentiated products}. In this case, the Cournot model no longer makes sense because the sum of quantities demanded for differentiated products is meaningless.

Let $c$ be the cost of production and $a$ be the demand-side reservation price. We shall consider the model through the perspective of pricing. Suppose that the two firms set the prices for their products at $p_1$ and $p_2$ respectively. Take the demand for the first firm's product as an example. We may assume that the demand is linear in price and that $b > 0$ is a coefficient representing the substitutability of the first firm's product for the second firm's. Consider
\begin{equation*}
    Q\left(p_1\right) = q_0 - kp_1,
\end{equation*}
where the $x$-intercept is $a$, so we can compute 
\begin{equation*}
    q_0 = ka.
\end{equation*}
Considering substitutability, we have 
\begin{equation*}
    q_1\left(p_1, p_2\right) = ka - kp_1 + bp_2.
\end{equation*}
\begin{dfnbox}{Bertrand Model of Duopoly}{Bertrand}
    Let $S_1, S_2 \coloneqq \left[0, \infty\right)$ be possible prices for two firms producing differentiated products. Let $c$ and $a$ be the cost of production and demand-side reservation price respectively. Assuming linear demand, then there exists positive constant $k$ and $b$ such that 
    \begin{align*}
        \pi_i\left(p_1, p_2\right) & = q_i\left(p_1, p_2\right)p_i - cq_i\left(p_1, p_2\right) \\
        & = \left(ka - kp_1 + bp_2\right)\left(p_i - c\right)
    \end{align*}
    for $i = 1, 2$, where $p_1 \in S_1, p_2 \in S_2$ are pricing strategies. The $2$-player normal-form game~$G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \pi_1, \pi_2\right\}$ is known as the {\color{red} \textbf{Bertrand model of duopoly}}. 
\end{dfnbox}
Via a similar process, we can compute the best responses and Nash equilibrium for the model.
\begin{probox}{Nash Equilibrium in Bertrand Model of Duopoly}{BertrandEqui}
    Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \pi_1, \pi_2\right\}$ be a Bertrand model of duopoly, then for any $p_2 \in S_2$, the best response of the first firm is
    \begin{equation*}
        R_1\left(p_2\right) = \frac{1}{2k}\left(ka + bp_2 + kc\right)
    \end{equation*} 
    and the Nash equilibrium is 
    \begin{equation*}
        p_1^* = p_2^* = \frac{ka + kc}{2k - b}.
    \end{equation*}
\end{probox}
\begin{notebox}
    \begin{remark}
        Note that this means that this model is sensible only if $b > 2k$.
    \end{remark}
\end{notebox}
We can use Bertrand model to analyse homogeneous product as well by setting $b = 0$. Assume that the consumers always buy from the firm which offers a lower price, then the demand for the first firm is 
\begin{equation*}
    q_1\left(p_1, p_2\right) = \begin{cases}
        ka - kp_1 & \quad\textrm{if } p_1 < p_2 \\
        0 & \quad\textrm{if } p_1 > p_2 \\
        \frac{ka - kp_1}{2} & \quad\textrm{if } p_1 = p_2
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation*}
The payoff can be computed as profit for the first firm by 
\begin{equation*}
    \pi_1\left(p_1, p_2\right) = \begin{cases}
        \left(ka - kp_1\right)\left(p_1 - c\right) & \quad\textrm{if } p_1 < p_2 \\
        0 & \quad\textrm{if } p_1 > p_2 \\
        \frac{1}{2}\left(ka - kp_1\right)\left(p_1 - c\right) & \quad\textrm{if } p_1 = p_2
    \end{cases}.
\end{equation*}
\begin{probox}{\small Nash Equilibrium of Betrand Model for Homogeneous Product}{homoNashEquiBetrand}
    The Nash equilibrium for the Bertrand model if both firms produce the same product is~$p_1 = p_2 = c$ where $c$ is the cost of production.
    \tcblower
    \begin{proof}
        It suffices to consider the first firm. Notice that when $p_1 < c$ or $p_1 > a$, we have $\pi_1\left(p_1, p_2\right) < 0$, so it suffices to consider the strategy space as $\left[c, a\right]$, where we assume that $c \leq a$. We consider $3$ cases. If $p_2 > \frac{a + c}{2}$, it is obvious that $p_1 = \frac{a + c}{2}$ is the best response. If $c < p_2 \leq \frac{a + c}{2}$, it is clear that 
        \begin{equation*}
            \sup\pi_1\left(p_1, p_2\right) = \left(ka - kp_2\right)\left(p_2 - c\right)
        \end{equation*}
        but there is no $p_1 \in \left[c, a\right]$ such that $\pi_1\left(p_1, p_2\right) = \left(ka - kp_2\right)\left(p_2 - c\right)$, and so there is no best response. If $p_2 = c$, then since $\pi_1\left(p_1, c\right) = 0$ for all $p_1 \in \left[c, a\right]$, we have~$R_1\left(p_2\right) = \left[c, a\right]$. Observe that $R_1\left(\left[c, a\right]\right) \cap R_2\left(\left[c, a\right]\right) = \left\{\left(c, c\right)\right\}$, so the only Nash equilibrium is $(c, c)$.
    \end{proof}
\end{probox}
An important implication of this is that if consumers only buy from the cheaper supplier, then the firms have no choice but to produce at the cost without any profit.

Another classic claim in economics is that public goods tend to be under-supplied in a free market. This is summarised as the \textit{Tragedy of the Commons}, which states the following problem:
\begin{quote}
    Suppose that there are $n$ persons ($n \geq 2$) in a community. Let $x_i$ be the amount of a public resource used by the $i$-th person and denote 
    \begin{equation*}
        X \coloneqq \sum_{i = 1}^nx_i.
    \end{equation*}
    For each unit usage of the resource, there is a fixed cost of $c$. The public resource has a total capacity of $X_{\max}$. When $x$ units of the resources are used, each unit induces a utility of $v\left(x\right)$, where $v$ is a strictly concave function for $x < X_{\max}$ and $v\left(x\right) = 0$ for $x \geq X_{\max}$. All the persons simultaneously choose how much of the public resource they wish to use.
\end{quote}
If we model this as an $n$-player normal-form game, the strategy space for the $i$-th person will be~$S_i \coloneqq \left[0, X_{\max}\right)$ and the payoff function is 
\begin{equation*}
    u_i\left(x_i, \mathbfit{x}_{-i}\right) \coloneqq x_iv\left(\sum_{k = 1}^{n}x_k\right) - cx_i.
\end{equation*}
Let $\mathbfit{x}^* \coloneqq \left(x_1^*, x_2^*, \cdots, x_n^*\right)$ be a Nash equilibrium, then clearly
\begin{equation*}
    \left.\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}\right\rvert_{\mathbfit{x} = \mathbfit{x}^*} = v\left(\sum_{k = 1}^{n}x_k^*\right) + x_i^*\left.\frac{\d v}{\d x_i}\right\rvert_{x_i = x_i^*} - c = 0
\end{equation*}
for each $i = 1, 2, \cdots, n$. If we use 
\begin{equation*}
    v\left(x\right) \coloneqq \begin{cases}
        0 & \quad\textrm{if } x \geq X_{\max} \\
        a - x^2 & \quad\textrm{if } x < X_{\max}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation*}
for some $a > c$ as the utility function, we have 
\begin{equation*}
    a - X^2 - 2x_i^*X - c = 0
\end{equation*}
for each $i = 1, 2, \cdots, n$. Intuitively, $x_1^* = x_2^* = \cdots = x_n^*$ because all payoff functions are the same, so 
\begin{equation*}
    a - n^2{x_i^*}^2 - 2n{x_i^*}^2 - c = 0
\end{equation*}
for each $i = 1, 2, \cdots, n$. Solving the equation yields $x_i^* = \sqrt{\frac{a - c}{n^2 + 2n}}$ for each $i = 1, 2, \cdots, n$ as the Nash equilibrium. 

Next, define the \textit{social welfare} as
\begin{equation*}
    w\left(x\right) = xv\left(x\right) - xc
\end{equation*}
where $x$ is the total usage of the public resource. Let $x^* < X_{\max}$ be the optimal usage for maximum social welfare and $\left(x_1^*, x_2^*, \cdots, x_n^*\right)$ be the Nash equilibrium. We claim that
\begin{equation*}
    x^* < \sum_{i = 1}^{n}x_i^*.
\end{equation*}
Denote $X^* \coloneqq \sum_{i = 1}^{n}x_i^* < X_{\max}$. Suppose on contrary that $x^* \geq X^*$, then we have 
\begin{equation*}
    v\left(X^*\right) \geq v\left(x^*\right) \quad\textrm{and}\quad 0 > v'\left(X^*\right) \geq v'\left(x^*\right)
\end{equation*}
since $v$ is strictly concave on $\left(-\infty, X_{\max}\right)$. Note that $n \geq 2$, so $\frac{X^*}{n} < X^* \leq x^*$, which means that 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{X^*}{n}v'\left(X^*\right) \geq \frac{X^*}{n}v'\left(x^*\right) > x^*v'\left(x^*\right).
\end{equation*}
Therefore, 
\begin{equation*}
    v\left(X^*\right) + \frac{X^*}{n}v'\left(X^*\right) > v\left(x^*\right) + x^*v'\left(x^*\right).
\end{equation*}
However, notice that since $x^*$ achieves maximum social welfare, we have 
\begin{align*}
    w'\left(x^*\right) & = v\left(x^*\right) + x^*v'\left(x^*\right) - c = 0.
\end{align*}
For each $i = 1, 2, \cdots, n$, we also have $x_i^* = \frac{X^*}{n}$ and thus
\begin{equation*}
    v\left(X^*\right) + \frac{X^*}{n}v'\left(X^*\right) - c = 0.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, $v\left(X^*\right) + \frac{X^*}{n}v'\left(X^*\right) = v\left(x^*\right) + x^*v'\left(x^*\right)$, which is a contradiction. The above argument precisely shows that at the Nash equilibrium, the usage of public resources always exceed what is socially optimal.
\section{Dynamic Games of Complete Information}
In static games, the players move independently and the strategy of one player will not affect the context in which another player would make decisions. However, there are many games where this is not true. For example, in a turn-based game, the sequence in which the players move makes a significant impact on the strategy of each player. Furthermore, such games consist of multiple stages and so a player has to observe the past actions in order to predict the best decision.

Note that now, the extent to which a player can observe the past is important as this impacts how much information the player can acquire to make a decision in his or her own profit.
\subsection{Perfect Information}
We will first discuss the first case where the information about the past moves are fully available.
\begin{dfnbox}{Dynamic Games of Complete and Perfect Information}{dynamicGame}
    A {\color{red} \textbf{dynamic game}} of {\color{red} \textbf{complete}} and {\color{red} \textbf{perfect}} information is a game where 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the players move in sequence,
        \item all previous moves are observed before the next move is taken, and
        \item payoffs are common knowledge to all players.
    \end{itemize}
\end{dfnbox}
Such games can be presented with a \textit{game tree} consisting of game states after each possible sequence of moves. A game state can be modelled by an ordered $n$-tuple $\left(x_{k1}, x_{k2}, \cdots, x_{kn}\right)$ denoting the payoffs off all players after $k$ moves. In this respect, the initial state can be represented by $\mathbf{0}$. Let $G \coloneqq \left\{S_1, S_2, \cdots, S_n, u_1, u_2, \cdots, u_n\right\}$ be such a dynamic game. 

Let $\mathbf{0}$ be the root of the game tree. For each payoff vector $\mathbfit{u}_{h - 1}$ in height $h - 1$, the $h$-th player takes a move in the \textit{feasible set} $A_h$ to modify the payoffs to a new payoff vector~$\mathbfit{u}_h$ and create an edge $s_h \coloneqq \mathbfit{u}_{h - 1}$-$\mathbfit{u}_h$. Consider the leaves of the tree formed this way at height~$n$. Note that every strategy is uniquely represented as the $\mathbf{0}$-$\mathbfit{u}$ path for some leaf $\mathbfit{u}$, where the projection~$\pi_i\left(\mathbfit{u}\right) = u_i\left(s_1, s_2, \cdots, s_n\right)$ is the payoff corresponding to the strategy.
